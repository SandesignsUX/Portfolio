Fuel Station Management — A Digital Transformation
The product covers four distinct user roles — cashier, accountant, owner, and admin — each with their own flows and data responsibilities.
01 — The problem
The problem nobody had named
The brief said "digitise operations." The site visit said something different.
Fuel station staff were running a business on paper logs, Excel, and Tally — three tools that didn't talk to each other.
What this caused
  • Cashiers manually entered the same data 3 times across disconnected tools
  • Owners had no live visibility without asking the accountant directly
  • Cashiers stayed an unpaid extra hour after every shift — nobody had flagged it
  • Tax season meant rushed, error-prone manual reconciliation
The real problem
  • Not just "replace paper with software" — eliminate the hidden workflow inefficiencies nobody had named yet
The real problem wasn't the tools — it was the workflow those tools had created.

Research didn't just inform the design — it expanded the brief. Two significant problems we solved were never mentioned until we went on-site.

04 — Wireframes
Straight to hi-fi — by design
With less than 3 weeks to dev handoff, lo-fi wireframes would have added a round of abstract feedback we couldn't afford.
Android app — 8 cashier flows across Fuel Summary, Oil Summary, CNG, Credit A/c, Cash Packets, Bank Transfers, Loss Reports, and Daily Rates.
The decision to go hi-fi immediately meant every feedback round was grounded in real interface decisions — not abstract boxes. The client could react to actual layout, hierarchy, and flow from day one.
Web dashboard — cashier data entry, daily sale reports, tank summaries, tax reports, and bank transfer management.
The 8-tile home screen was a deliberate information architecture decision — cashiers needed to reach any flow in one tap. Depth was hidden behind each tile, not exposed on the home screen.
05 — Key decisions
What I chose and why
Why I skipped lo-fi and went straight to hi-fi
ConstraintLess than 3 weeks to dev handoff — lo-fi would have added a round of abstract feedback we couldn't afford
RejectedLo-fi wireframes — too slow, feedback too abstract for non-designer stakeholders
ChoseHi-fi wireframes from day one — gave the client something concrete to react to, kept the dev team unblocked

Less than 3 weeks to dev handoff. Lo-fi would have added a feedback round we couldn't afford. Hi-fi gave everyone something concrete from day one. The constraint forced a better process.

Why I chose real-time alerts over end-of-day reports
FoundBy the time an end-of-day report flagged an error, it had already affected multiple downstream entries
RejectedEnd-of-day summary report — too late, errors had already compounded
ChoseField-level inline alerts — catch mismatches at entry, before they propagate

The accountant made it clear during testing — by the time an end-of-day report flagged an error, it had already affected downstream entries. Real-time caught it at the source.

Why we built a dedicated credit invoicing flow
Found6-step manual invoice process uncovered during flow mapping — not in original scope
ChoseDedicated automated flow — replaced the entire WhatsApp-based process with a single action

Six manual steps. Not in the original brief. Clearly broken. I made the case to include it — one automated flow replaced the entire WhatsApp-based process.

Why I stayed involved through development
FoundGST calculations, credit interest, tax report logic — too complex to hand off via Figma files alone
ChoseDaily calls through development — clarified APIs, reviewed builds, adjusted designs to match technical realities

Handing off and stepping away wasn't an option. The business logic was complex enough that developers needed ongoing design input throughout the build. The handoff was a collaboration, not a delivery.

06 — Prototype & testing
Tested where it actually mattered
At the fuel station, during actual shift-end, with real users on a staging build.
How
  • Moderated testing at the fuel station
  • Real users, live data, staging platform
  • One-on-one with each cashier manager
What we found
  • Accountant flows completed independently
  • Fuel summary needed guidance
  • 3/4 cashiers reached for paper mid-task
  • Blank forms broke mental models
What changed
  • Added prefilled forms from previous day
  • Cross-period dashboard comparisons
  • Reduced redundant navigation steps
  • Real-time field-level error alerts
How we tested
We tested at the fuel station during actual shift-end — real users, live data, staging platform. No meeting room, no simulated tasks.
The most telling moment
3 out of 4 cashiers reached for their paper sheet mid-task to check the previous day's closing balance. The app had blank forms where it should have had data. We'd designed for what the system needed — not what users needed.
What changed
Prefilled forms auto-populated from the previous day's closing data. Dashboard improvements for cross-period comparisons. A tighter navigation structure based on where friction actually appeared.
Final screens after testing iterations — cashier fuel summary (mobile), data entry + tank grid (web), oil summary (mobile).
The pre-filled form pattern is visible here — shift date, shift number, and tank pre-selected based on the cashier's previous entry. One of the changes that came directly from watching users reach for their paper sheet.
We'd designed for what the system needed from users — not what users needed from the system. Testing caught it.

08 — Reflection & foresight
Honest reflection
What went wrong
Initial cashier flows had friction — designed around system logic, not user mental models. Post-launch GST and credit interest edge cases only surfaced in live transactions.
What I'd do differently
Run a cognitive walkthrough with the accountant before usability testing. Involve engineering earlier in complex calculation logic. Explore connecting fuel pumps directly to eliminate manual entry at source.

The cashier flows had more friction than expected — we'd designed for what the system needed, not what users needed. Testing caught it and we iterated before launch.


Post-launch, a 2-week refinement phase surfaced edge cases only visible in real transactions. No full redesigns — but it confirmed staged testing doesn't replace live use.


If I did this again: cognitive walkthrough with the accountant before testing, engineering involved earlier in the business logic, and fuel pump integration to eliminate manual cashier entry entirely.

You do not need to have done everything perfectly. You need to show you know what you would do next.
